\section{Weight-Orthogonalization Baseline}
\label{app:weight-ortho}

As an additional ablation we applied Arditi-style weight
orthogonalization on all $L \times E = 1024$ expert down-projection
matrices: $W_{\ell,e}^{\text{down}} \leftarrow W - \hat{d}\hat{d}^\top W$
where $\hat{d} = \dbeh[\ell]/\|\dbeh[\ell]\|$. Results on the same
attack suite are reported in Table~\ref{tab:wortho}. The intervention
is near-neutral — it marginally improves \emph{none} ($+6$pp) but
provides no help on PAIR or past-tense. Together
with the low-cost advantage of our test-time method, this justifies
focusing the main paper on router-bias rather than weight-level
interventions.

\section{Judge Configuration}
\label{app:judge}

We follow PandaGuard's~\cite{pandaguard} LLM-judge methodology rather than lexical refusal-string matching, since string matching misses partial compliance and surface-paraphrased refusals. PandaGuard offers three LLM-based judges in the Qwen, Llama, and GPT families and reports moderate agreement among them, with Qwen2.5 occupying the median sensitivity. We adopt Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct~\cite{qwen25} as the single judge for all methods on all backbones in this paper.

\paragraph{Safety prompt.} For the six jailbreak attacks in Table~\ref{tab:main}, the judge sees the harmful goal and the model's response and emits an integer rubric score $r \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 100\}$, anchored at $r{=}0$ = ``promotes harm, violence, exploitation,'' $r{=}50$ = ``neutral,'' and $r{=}100$ = ``helpful, ethical, promotes wellbeing,'' with the prompt explicitly telling the judge that refusals and helpful ethical advice both score $90$--$100$. We then rescale to an alignment score $s = r/100 \in [0, 1]$ and label a response \textsc{safe} when $s \geq 0.7$. The threshold $0.7$ (clearly leaning toward refusal rather than partial compliance) is stricter than thresholds commonly used in attack-side leaderboards, reflecting that we are scoring a defense, where partial compliance should not count as safe.

\paragraph{Over-refusal prompt.} For XSTest, the same judge sees the benign-but-superficially-risky prompt and the response, and emits a binary label $\ell \in \{\textsc{answer}, \textsc{refuse}\}$, anchored at $\textsc{answer}$ = ``the assistant attempted to address the question (even partially)'' and $\textsc{refuse}$ = ``the assistant declined, apologized, warned, or redirected away.'' The prompt explicitly informs the judge that the question is benign so that the judge does not penalise the assistant for engaging with surface-risky language. The label is parsed from the judge's first matched token via a case-insensitive regex matching either \texttt{ANSWER} or \texttt{REFUSE}; the XSTest answer rate is the fraction of responses with $\ell = \textsc{answer}$.

\paragraph{Cross-method fairness.} The same judge model, prompt templates, and threshold are applied identically to \emph{Original}, \emph{SteerMoE}, and \name on every backbone. Cross-method differences therefore reflect the methods themselves rather than any judge-side asymmetry.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:impl}

\paragraph{Router hook.} OLMoE's gate module returns either a raw logit
tensor (prefill) or a $(p, \text{top\_val}, \text{top\_idx})$ tuple
(decode with KV cache). Our hook handles both by recomputing the bias
addition before softmax and re-extracting top-$k$.

\paragraph{Decoding config.} Greedy decoding throughout (\texttt{do\_sample=False}, \texttt{max\_new\_tokens=256}, \texttt{temperature=1.0}, bf16); \texttt{pad\_token\_id} is set to \texttt{eos\_token\_id}. The same decoding config is applied to \emph{Original}, \emph{SteerMoE}, and \name to ensure cross-method comparability.

\paragraph{Direction normalization.} Both offline directions $\drefuse$ and $\dbeh$ are unit-normalized per layer (each $\drefuse[\ell]$ and $\dbeh[\ell]$ is divided by its $\ell_2$ norm) and stored once per backbone, so $\lambda(q) = h(q) \cdot \drefuse[\ell^*]$ and $s(q) = h(q) \cdot \dbeh[\ell^*]$ are scale-invariant projections.

\paragraph{Calibration of the modulation ramp.} The endpoints $(\lambda_{\text{lo}}, \lambda_{\text{hi}})$ are set per backbone on a held-out split disjoint from the test prompts: $\lambda_{\text{lo}}$ at the $95$th percentile of $\lambda(q)$ on the benign half (so $95\%$ of benign queries fall below it and trigger no intervention), $\lambda_{\text{hi}}$ at the $5$th percentile on the risky half (so $95\%$ of risky queries fall above it and receive the full bias). Calibration is one-shot at offline time and does not depend on the test query.

\paragraph{Compute.} All experiments run on a single NVIDIA B200 ($180$\,GB). Offline extraction of $\drefuse$ and $\dbeh$ takes ${\sim}10$ minutes per backbone at $N{=}200$ contrast pairs. The online phase adds one forward-backward pass per query before generation, accounting for ${\sim}5\%$ of the cost of $256$-token greedy generation. End-to-end main-table generation takes ${\sim}70$ minutes per $200$-query evaluation cell, and judging takes ${\sim}10$ minutes per $200$ responses. Total compute for the full experimental sweep across three backbones, six attacks, and three baselines fits in under $200$ B200-hours, putting the entire pipeline within reach of a single-GPU node.

\section{Probe-Layer Sweep}
\label{app:probe_layer}

The two columns ``safe rate'' and ``XSTest'' isolate the two channels through which $\ell^*$ enters the method. Safe rate captures the quality of the identification gradient (independent of the modulation signal, since $\rho(\lambda)$ saturates to $1$ on risky calibration prompts). XSTest captures the quality of $\lambda(q)$ as a benign-vs-risky gauge, since on benign prompts the identified set is irrelevant once $\lambda(q)$ falls below $\lambda_{\text{lo}}$. Identification saturates over a wide mid-layer plateau ($\ell^* \in \{10, 13\}$), while modulation is sharply peaked at the middle. This dissociation is what motivates ``select where modulation peaks within the identification plateau,'' giving $\ell^* = 10$.
